Build listening accuracy through a repeatable evidence loop rather than repeated exposure alone. The purpose of this page is to help learners notice **why** an answer was missed, classify the error, and design the next practice attempt around that specific weakness.

## The LBFL listening cycle

Use the same five-step sequence for each short practice set:

1. **Predict** — read the task before listening and anticipate the kind of information that could fill each gap or answer each question.
2. **Listen for structure** — follow changes in topic, contrast, examples, sequence, correction, and conclusion rather than trying to remember every word.
3. **Commit to an answer** — write what the evidence supports while the recording is active. Avoid repeatedly changing an answer only because another phrase sounds familiar.
4. **Review the evidence** — compare the answer with the transcript or model and identify the exact phrase that confirmed or contradicted your choice.
5. **Retry deliberately** — repeat a similar task with one correction target in mind.

The goal is not merely to count correct answers. The useful question is: **What listening decision produced the error?**

## Five common error patterns to diagnose

### 1. Prediction failure

Sometimes the learner begins listening without deciding what kind of information is required. Before the recording starts, inspect the grammar and context around a gap or question. Ask whether the likely answer is a person, place, number, date, action, cause, comparison, or descriptive detail.

A prediction is not a guess at the final answer. It is a way to reduce unnecessary processing while listening.

### 2. Paraphrase failure

The wording in a question may not be repeated exactly in the audio. Train yourself to connect equivalent meanings.

For example, a task may refer to a **decrease**, while the speaker describes something that **fell**, **declined**, or became **lower**. The useful skill is recognizing the relationship between expressions, not memorizing one wording.

After reviewing a task, record the question phrase and the matching phrase from the transcript. This creates a personal paraphrase bank.

### 3. Distractor capture

A speaker may mention one option and then correct, qualify, reject, or replace it. If you commit too early, the first familiar phrase can become a distractor.

Listen for language that changes the direction of meaning, such as contrast, correction, preference, exception, or revision. When reviewing, identify the point at which the final meaning became clear.

### 4. Detail-loss failure

A learner may understand the general topic but miss the exact detail required for a form, note, label, number, name, or sequence.

For these errors, practise short sections with a narrow target. Instead of replaying an entire passage, identify the few seconds surrounding the missed detail and analyse what prevented recognition: speed, spelling, unfamiliar pronunciation, divided attention, or weak prediction.

### 5. Transfer failure

Sometimes the correct meaning is heard but transferred incorrectly to the answer sheet or notes. The problem may be spelling, singular/plural form, number format, or writing an answer that does not fit the sentence.

Treat transfer as a separate skill. During review, check both **meaning** and **answer form**.

## A 20-minute focused practice session

Use this compact routine when you do not have time for a full practice test.

| Time | Action | Evidence to keep |
|---|---|---|
| 2 minutes | Preview the task and predict answer types | Short prediction notes |
| 6 minutes | Listen once under normal conditions | First answers only |
| 4 minutes | Review transcript/model evidence | Exact confirming phrases |
| 4 minutes | Classify each error | Error type + cause |
| 4 minutes | Retry a selected segment or similar task | Corrected response |

Do not spend the whole session replaying the audio. The review stage is where practice becomes deliberate learning.

## Build a listening error log

After each session, record only mistakes that teach you something. A useful entry contains four parts:

| Field | Example |
|---|---|
| Question type | Note completion |
| What went wrong | Heard the idea but missed the number |
| Error category | Detail loss |
| Next correction | Predict number format and listen for unit words |

Over time, the log should reveal repeated patterns. If most errors come from paraphrase recognition, another full practice test may be less useful than a short paraphrase-focused drill. If errors cluster around names and numbers, practise those separately.

## Foundation practice areas

Use the cycle above across these listening tasks:

- main idea and supporting details;
- form and note completion;
- multiple-choice listening;
- map and diagram labelling;
- summary completion;
- sequence and process listening;
- paraphrase recognition;
- names, numbers, dates and spelling.

## Self-review questions

After a practice attempt, answer these questions before moving on:

- What information did I correctly predict before listening?
- Which answer was lost because I did not recognize a paraphrase?
- Did I choose an option before the speaker finished correcting or qualifying the idea?
- Which mistake came from hearing, and which came from writing or spelling?
- What single correction will I test in the next attempt?

The purpose of the review is to convert a vague result such as “my listening is weak” into a testable learning target.

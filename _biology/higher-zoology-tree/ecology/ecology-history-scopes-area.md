# Ecology: History, Scope and Branches

This Lecture 01 forms part of the canonical 29-lecture LBFL Ecology course. It develops ecology: history, scope and branches through ecological mechanisms, evidence, interpretation and application.

## Learning Objectives

- Explain the scope of ecology and major stages in its historical development.
- Distinguish organism, population, community, ecosystem, landscape, and biosphere levels.
- Compare autecology, population ecology, community ecology, ecosystem ecology, landscape ecology, and applied ecology.
- Explain why ecology depends on physiology, evolution, genetics, geology, climatology, geography, and statistics.

## 1. Ecology as the science of relationships

Ecology is the scientific study of interactions among organisms and between organisms and their physical environment. It asks more than where a species occurs. Ecologists ask why it occurs there, which resources or conditions limit it, how other species alter its abundance, how energy and matter move through the system, and how disturbance changes biological organization.

```text
Organism → Population → Community → Ecosystem → Landscape → Biosphere
             pattern, process, interaction, feedback, change
```

A pattern observed at one level can be produced by processes operating at another. The burrowing behaviour of a crab, for example, is an organism-level trait, but the collective activity of thousands of crabs can alter sediment aeration and nutrient dynamics at ecosystem scale.

## 2. Historical development

Early natural history provided careful descriptions of organisms and habitats. Modern ecology added hypothesis testing, experiments, long-term monitoring, mathematical models, and spatial analysis. Alexander von Humboldt helped establish the importance of climate in explaining geographic vegetation patterns. Ernst Haeckel popularized the term *Ökologie* in the nineteenth century for the study of organism–environment relationships. Later developments in population growth, competition, food-web theory, ecosystem energetics, biogeochemistry, conservation biology, and global-change ecology expanded the field.

The purpose of learning this history is not to memorize names. It is to understand the progression from **description → mechanism → prediction → management**.

## 4. Scope and branches

**Autecology** focuses on the response of an organism or species to its environment. **Population ecology** studies density, birth, death, dispersal, and growth. **Community ecology** examines diversity and species interactions. **Ecosystem ecology** studies energy flow, productivity, decomposition, and nutrient cycling. **Landscape ecology** emphasizes spatial pattern, patches, corridors, and matrices. **Applied ecology** uses ecological knowledge in conservation, fisheries, wildlife management, restoration, agriculture, pollution control, and ecosystem management.

## 5. Ecology is interdisciplinary

Physiology explains tolerance mechanisms. Genetics and evolution explain variation and adaptation. Geology and geomorphology shape substrates and landscapes. Climatology describes temperature and rainfall regimes. Statistics separates biological signal from sampling noise. GIS and remote sensing reveal spatial patterns. Economics and social science help explain how human decisions alter ecosystems.

## Bangladesh Context

Bangladesh contains floodplains, haors, beels, river chars, hill forests, coastal mudflats, mangroves, agricultural systems, and dense urban ecosystems within a small geographic area. These systems provide strong examples for studying scale, interaction, disturbance, and ecosystem management.

## Application Exercise

Choose one local ecosystem and write separate ecological questions at organism, population, community, ecosystem, and landscape levels. Explain how changing scale changes the question.

## Common Misconceptions

- Ecology is not only the study of pollution; it includes fundamental interactions and processes.
- Habitat and ecosystem are not interchangeable concepts.

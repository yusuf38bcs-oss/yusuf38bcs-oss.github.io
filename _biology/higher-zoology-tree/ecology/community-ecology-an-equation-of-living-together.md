# Community Ecology: Living Together

This Lecture 13 forms part of the canonical 29-lecture LBFL Ecology course. It develops community ecology: living together through ecological mechanisms, evidence, interpretation and application.

{% include education/framework-links.html %}

## Learning Objectives and Outcomes — LOLO

- Describe community composition, dominance, physiognomy, and trophic structure.
- Distinguish species richness, evenness, and diversity.
- Explain ecotones and edge effects without assuming that all edges increase diversity.
- Relate vertical and horizontal stratification to environmental gradients.

## 1. Community structure

A community is an assemblage of populations that occur together and interact. Community structure includes more than a species list. It includes relative abundance, dominance, body-size distribution, vertical layering, trophic organization, and the pattern of interactions among species.

## 2. Richness, evenness, and diversity

**Species richness, \(S\),** is the number of species present. **Evenness** describes how equally individuals are distributed among those species. **Diversity** combines information about richness and relative abundance.

Two forests can each contain ten species, yet differ greatly in diversity if one forest is dominated by a single species while the other has a more even abundance distribution.

## 3. Dominance

A dominant species contributes strongly to abundance, biomass, basal area, or cover. Dominance should not be confused with the keystone concept. A keystone species can have a disproportionately large ecological effect even when it is not abundant.

## 4. Stratification

Forests may contain canopy, understory, shrub, herb, and litter layers. Lakes have littoral, pelagic, and benthic zones. Soil communities also vary with depth. Stratification reflects gradients in light, oxygen, moisture, temperature, and resources.

## 5. Ecotones and edge effects

An **ecotone** is a transition between ecological communities or ecosystem types. Edges can differ from interiors in light, wind, temperature, predator access, recruitment, and species composition. Edge effects can be positive or negative. It is therefore unsafe to assume that every edge automatically increases biodiversity.

## Integrated Perspective

### 3. Interaction networks

Species do not interact equally strongly with every other species. A few strong interactions can dominate short-term dynamics, while weak interactions may contribute to stability by distributing effects across the network. Food-web thinking therefore goes beyond a single linear chain.

## Bangladesh Context

Forest–tea garden edges, wetland–cropland boundaries, riverbank–settlement interfaces, and mangrove–mudflat transitions provide accessible examples of ecotones and edge effects.

## Learning Activity and Application — LALA

Survey a small community and record richness, the most abundant species, visible vertical layers, and any edge–interior difference. Write a short structural description.

## Common Misconceptions

- High richness does not guarantee high evenness.
- Dominant species and keystone species are not synonyms.

## Critical-Thinking Questions

1. How would you explain the central mechanism in Community Ecology I: Structure, Species Diversity, Ecotones and Stratification without relying on memorized definitions?
2. Describe community composition, dominance, physiognomy, and trophic structure.
3. Relate vertical and horizontal stratification to environmental gradients.
4. Design a field observation or experiment that could test one claim from this lecture.
5. What uncertainty or trade-off would matter most when applying this concept to ecosystem management?

# Terrestrial and Aquatic Ecosystem Patterns

This Lecture 25 forms part of the canonical 29-lecture LBFL Ecology course. It develops terrestrial and aquatic ecosystem patterns through ecological mechanisms, evidence, interpretation and application.

## Learning Objectives

- Explain how climate change interacts with land-use change, pollution, and biological invasion.
- Distinguish resistance, resilience, recovery, and transformation.
- Connect carbon footprints and mitigation to ecosystem management.
- Explain pollutant exposure, bioaccumulation, biomagnification, and terrestrial–aquatic pathways.

## 1. Global change is a multi-driver problem

Climate warming, altered rainfall, sea-level rise, ocean change, land-use conversion, nutrient loading, chemical pollution, and biological invasion often act together. Single-stressor predictions may therefore underestimate ecological responses.

## 2. Ecological responses

Possible responses include range shifts, altered phenology, changes in productivity, mortality events, trophic mismatches, altered disease dynamics, and changes in disturbance regimes. Outcomes depend on exposure, sensitivity, dispersal, and adaptive capacity.

## 7. Terrestrial and aquatic pathways

Contaminants can alter soil organisms and plant growth, move into waterways through runoff, accumulate in sediments, or trigger eutrophication and oxygen depletion. Effective management therefore follows pollutants across connected land–water systems.

## Integrated Perspective

### 4. Environmental gradients and zonation

Coasts, mountain slopes, lake depths, and forest edges contain gradual changes in environmental conditions. Species tolerance, dispersal, competition, and disturbance combine to produce zonation. A biological boundary should not automatically be attributed to a single factor.

## Integrated Perspective

## 1. Chars as dynamic landforms

Riverine and estuarine chars in the Bengal delta are created by sediment deposition and reshaped by erosion. A char should therefore be treated as a dynamic geomorphic surface rather than a permanently fixed island.

## 2. From bare sediment to vegetation

Fresh deposits may be frequently inundated, unstable, low in organic matter, and difficult for plants to colonize. Pioneer vegetation can trap sediment, reduce surface movement, and add organic matter. These changes can facilitate later colonists. Flooding or erosion can interrupt or reverse the sequence.

## 3. A mosaic of successional stages

Different parts of a char can have different ages and disturbance histories. High ground, low swales, eroding banks, newly accreted tips, grazing areas, plantations, and cultivated zones may occur side by side.

```text
new mudflat → pioneer vegetation → mixed cover → older patch
       ↘ erosion / flood / deposition ↗
```

This creates a landscape mosaic rather than one uniform successional stage.

## Bangladesh Context

Coastal salinity, cyclone exposure, wetland conversion, urban heat, water pollution, and sea-level change interact rather than occurring as isolated problems. Integrated management must link upstream and downstream processes, land use, biodiversity, livelihoods, and monitoring.

## Application Exercise

Choose a Bangladesh ecosystem and build a Driver → Mechanism → Indicator → Mitigation/Adaptation matrix containing climate, land-use, and pollution drivers. Add uncertainty and trade-offs.

## Common Misconceptions

- Climate change is not the only driver of environmental degradation.
- Not every chemical pollutant biomagnifies.
- Resilience is not the same as remaining unchanged during disturbance.

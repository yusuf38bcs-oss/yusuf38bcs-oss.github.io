# Mutualism, Commensalism and Facilitation

This Lecture 16 forms part of the canonical 29-lecture LBFL Ecology course. It develops mutualism, commensalism and facilitation through ecological mechanisms, evidence, interpretation and application.

{% include education/framework-links.html %}

## Learning Objectives and Outcomes — LOLO

- Classify major positive and negative species interactions.
- Compare competition, predation, herbivory, parasitism, mutualism, and facilitation.
- Explain how density and resource availability influence biotic limitation.
- Analyze direct and indirect human effects on interaction networks.

## 1. Interaction signs

Ecologists often summarize pairwise effects with signs.

| Interaction | Species A | Species B |
|---|---:|---:|
| Competition | − | − |
| Predation / herbivory | + | − |
| Parasitism | + | − |
| Mutualism | + | + |
| Commensalism | + | 0 |
| Amensalism | − | 0 |

The signs are a starting point, not a complete mechanism. Interaction strength changes with density, life stage, resources, and environmental conditions.

## 4. Positive interactions

Mutualism and facilitation can be especially important under stressful conditions. Pollinators increase plant reproductive success, mycorrhizal fungi improve nutrient acquisition, and nurse plants can provide shade or moisture that improves seedling establishment.

## 5. Humans as ecological agents

Harvesting, hunting, fishing, pesticides, invasive species, habitat conversion, and pollution do more than alter the physical environment. They also restructure food webs, competition, and mutualistic relationships.

## Bangladesh Context

A rice-field food web can include crop plants, herbivorous insects, spiders, frogs, birds, microbes, and people. Broad-spectrum pesticide use may reduce pests and their natural enemies at the same time, changing later pest dynamics.

## Learning Activity and Application — LALA

Construct an interaction web for a pond or rice field with at least eight organisms. Label competition, predation, herbivory, parasitism, and mutualism, then predict the effect of removing one species.

## Common Misconceptions

- Mutualistic partners are not always obligately dependent on each other.
- Predators do not inevitably drive prey to extinction; feedbacks can stabilize or destabilize both populations.

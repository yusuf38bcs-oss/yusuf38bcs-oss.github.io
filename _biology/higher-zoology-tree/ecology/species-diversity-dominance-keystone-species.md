# Species Diversity, Dominance and Keystone Species

This Lecture 17 forms part of the canonical 29-lecture LBFL Ecology course. It develops species diversity, dominance and keystone species through ecological mechanisms, evidence, interpretation and application.

{% include education/framework-links.html %}

## Learning Objectives and Outcomes — LOLO

- Distinguish keystone, dominant, and foundation species.
- Explain direct and indirect effects in trophic cascades.
- Interpret strong and weak links in ecological interaction networks.
- Connect niche differentiation with mechanisms of coexistence.

## 1. Keystone species

A keystone species has an ecological effect that is disproportionately large relative to its abundance or biomass. Keystone effects can arise through predation, mutualism, ecosystem engineering, or control of a critical resource.

A **dominant species** is abundant or contributes a large share of biomass. A **foundation species** creates or maintains habitat structure. The same species may fit more than one category, but the terms are not synonyms.

## 2. Trophic cascades

A predator can reduce herbivore abundance or alter herbivore behaviour, indirectly changing plant biomass. The simplified sequence is:

```text
Predator ↑ → Herbivore ↓ → Plant biomass ↑
```

Real food webs contain omnivory, alternative prey, spatial refuges, and multiple predators, so cascade strength varies among systems.

## 3. Interaction networks

Species do not interact equally strongly with every other species. A few strong interactions can dominate short-term dynamics, while weak interactions may contribute to stability by distributing effects across the network. Food-web thinking therefore goes beyond a single linear chain.

## 4. Niche differentiation and coexistence

Stable coexistence can be supported by differences in resource use, timing, habitat, susceptibility to predators, or response to environmental variation. Spatial heterogeneity and disturbance can also prevent one competitor from excluding all others.

## 5. Ecosystem engineers

Some organisms alter the physical environment and thereby change resource availability for other species. Burrowing crabs, termites, earthworms, large herbivores, and reef-forming organisms can all act as ecosystem engineers in appropriate systems.

## Integrated Perspective

### 2. Richness, evenness, and diversity

**Species richness, \(S\),** is the number of species present. **Evenness** describes how equally individuals are distributed among those species. **Diversity** combines information about richness and relative abundance.

Two forests can each contain ten species, yet differ greatly in diversity if one forest is dominated by a single species while the other has a more even abundance distribution.

### 3. Dominance

A dominant species contributes strongly to abundance, biomass, basal area, or cover. Dominance should not be confused with the keystone concept. A keystone species can have a disproportionately large ecological effect even when it is not abundant.

## Bangladesh Context

Mangrove trees can act as habitat-forming foundation species, while predators, crabs, and decomposers occupy different interaction roles. Calling a species 'keystone' requires evidence of disproportionate ecological effect.

## Learning Activity and Application — LALA

Draw a six-species food web and remove one predator in a thought experiment. Predict one direct and two indirect consequences, then identify data that would test those predictions.

## Common Misconceptions

- A rare or endangered species is not automatically a keystone species.
- Food-web arrow direction must be defined before interpretation.

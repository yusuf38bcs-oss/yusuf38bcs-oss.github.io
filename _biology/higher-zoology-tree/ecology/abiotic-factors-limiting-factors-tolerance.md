# Abiotic Factors, Limiting Factors and Tolerance

This Lecture 04 forms part of the canonical 29-lecture LBFL Ecology course. It develops abiotic factors, limiting factors and tolerance through ecological mechanisms, evidence, interpretation and application.

## Learning Objectives

- Define a limiting factor and distinguish resource limitation from unsuitable environmental conditions.
- Compare Liebig's law of the minimum with Shelford's law of tolerance.
- Interpret optimum zones, zones of physiological stress, and limits of tolerance.
- Use evidence to identify a likely limiting factor in a real ecological scenario.

## 1. What is a limiting factor?

Growth, survival, reproduction, and distribution depend on many environmental variables, but they do not all constrain an organism equally at every moment. A **limiting factor** is a resource or condition whose shortage, excess, or unsuitable value places the strongest constraint on biological performance.

## 2. Liebig's law of the minimum

Liebig's principle became famous through plant nutrition. Growth may be limited by the essential resource that is least available relative to biological demand. The barrel analogy is useful: the shortest stave determines how much water the barrel can hold.

The principle is valuable, but real ecosystems are more complex. Resources interact, physiological demand changes with life stage, and removing one limitation may expose another.

## 3. Shelford's law of tolerance

Shelford extended the logic from shortage to both deficiency and excess. A species has a range of tolerance for an environmental factor.

```text
intolerance | stress | optimum | stress | intolerance
low -------------------------------------------- high
```

Performance is usually greatest in an optimum zone. In a zone of physiological stress, organisms may survive but grow or reproduce poorly. Beyond lower or upper limits, survival becomes impossible.

## 4. Ecological amplitude

Species with broad tolerances can occupy a wider range of environments than species with narrow tolerances. Tolerance also changes among life stages. Adults may tolerate a salinity or temperature that eggs, larvae, seedlings, or juveniles cannot.

## 5. Conditions versus resources

Temperature is primarily an environmental **condition** because organisms do not consume it. Food, nitrogen, water, or nesting space can function as **resources** because use by one organism may reduce availability to others.

## 6. How to demonstrate limitation

Correlation is not enough. Stronger evidence comes from a consistent gradient, experimental manipulation, appropriate controls, and replication across sites or times. A good ecological argument should therefore distinguish an observed association from a tested limiting mechanism.

## Integrated Perspective

### 1. Light as energy and information

For plants and photosynthetic microbes, light is a source of energy. For animals, it is also information used in orientation, foraging, predator avoidance, migration, reproduction, and biological timing. Important dimensions include intensity, wavelength, duration, and daily or seasonal timing.

A forest canopy intercepts much of the incoming radiation, producing low-light environments below. Aquatic systems show another gradient: light intensity falls with depth and different wavelengths are absorbed at different rates.

### 3. Temperature and biological rates

Temperature affects enzyme activity, membrane function, diffusion, development, oxygen demand, and water loss. Ectotherms depend strongly on environmental heat, whereas endotherms use metabolic heat to maintain a more stable body temperature.

Thermal performance usually rises toward an optimum and then declines, often sharply, as high temperatures disrupt proteins, membranes, water balance, or oxygen supply.

### 4. Microclimate

Weather-station temperature does not equal the temperature of a leaf surface, shaded soil, animal burrow, pond edge, or tree cavity. Small organisms can change exposure by selecting microhabitats. Distribution models therefore improve when relevant microclimate is considered.

## Integrated Perspective

## 1. Water as a resource and a medium

Terrestrial organisms require water for biochemical reactions, transport, cooling, and cellular hydration. Aquatic organisms live in water, but still face limits created by dissolved oxygen, salinity, flow, turbidity, and depth. Water balance reflects gains from uptake, drinking, food, or metabolism and losses through evaporation, respiration, and excretion.

## 2. Soil as an ecological system

Soil is a dynamic mixture of mineral particles, organic matter, air, water, and living organisms. **Texture** describes the proportions of sand, silt, and clay, while **structure** describes how particles form aggregates. Clay-rich soils often retain more water and ions but may drain slowly. Sandy soils drain rapidly.

Soil pH changes nutrient solubility, microbial activity, and toxicity. Organic matter improves nutrient retention, water-holding capacity, and the resource base for decomposers.

## 3. Salinity and osmoregulation

Salinity changes the osmotic environment. Freshwater animals and marine animals face different water and ion-balance problems. Estuarine species may be **euryhaline**, tolerating broad salinity variation, or **stenohaline**, restricted to a narrow range.

In plants, salinity lowers external water potential, may cause ion toxicity, and can interfere with nutrient acquisition. Mangrove species use combinations of salt exclusion, salt secretion, succulence, and selective ion regulation.

## Bangladesh Context

In ponds, rice fields, shrimp ghers, freshwater wetlands, and coastal estuaries, dissolved oxygen, temperature, salinity, nutrients, water depth, and food can become limiting at different times. The limiting factor in the dry season may differ from the limiting factor during the monsoon.

## Application Exercise

A pond has abundant food, but dissolved oxygen falls from 7 mg/L in the afternoon to 2 mg/L before dawn and fish mortality occurs near dawn. Propose a limiting-factor hypothesis and an experiment to test it.

## Common Misconceptions

- The limiting factor is not necessarily the factor with the lowest absolute concentration; limitation is relative to biological demand or tolerance.
- The same factor does not remain limiting in every season.

# Life-History Strategies and Ecological Trade-offs

This Lecture 11 forms part of the canonical 29-lecture LBFL Ecology course. It develops life-history strategies and ecological trade-offs through ecological mechanisms, evidence, interpretation and application.


## Key Ideas

- Distinguish density-dependent from density-independent population effects.
- Explain negative feedback in population regulation.
- Use r/K selection as a historical heuristic without treating it as a rigid classification.
- Explain life-history trade-offs among survival, growth, and reproduction.

## 1. Density dependence

Competition, disease transmission, territorial conflict, and some forms of predation often intensify as population density rises. When per-capita population growth declines with density, the resulting negative feedback can regulate population size.

## 2. Density-independent forces

Cyclones, floods, fire, extreme temperatures, and toxic spills can cause major mortality without depending directly on population density. Even so, exposure can still vary among habitats or social groups, so the label should be used carefully.

## 3. Limitation versus regulation

A limiting factor reduces growth or performance. A regulating process creates density-dependent feedback that tends to push population size toward a characteristic range. Every regulator limits growth at some stage, but not every limiting factor regulates a population.

## 4. r/K selection as a syllabus framework

The classical r/K framework associates rapid development, early reproduction, and many offspring with variable environments, while slower reproduction, larger investment per offspring, and stronger competitive ability are associated with populations near carrying capacity.

Modern life-history ecology treats this as a useful historical heuristic, not a strict two-box classification. Species vary along multiple trade-off axes: offspring size versus number, current versus future reproduction, and growth versus maintenance.

## 5. Delayed density dependence

If the effect of density appears after a time lag, populations can overshoot, oscillate, or cycle. Resource depletion in one season can alter recruitment in the next.

## Integrated Perspective

### 4. Plasticity and evolutionary change

Phenotypic plasticity allows the same genotype to produce different phenotypes in different environments. Evolutionary adaptation requires heritable variation and differential reproductive success across generations. Plasticity can be beneficial, neutral, or costly; it is not automatically adaptive.

## Bangladesh Context

Mosquitoes, crop pests, fish stocks, rodents, and wetland birds are not regulated by the same processes. Weather forcing, density dependence, disease, harvesting, and natural-enemy dynamics must be separated.

## Application and Practice

Classify food competition, cyclone mortality, contagious disease, nest-site shortage, and a sudden pesticide spill as density-dependent, density-independent, or context-dependent, and justify each decision.

## Common Misconceptions

- r-selected and K-selected are not rigid modern species boxes.
- Density-independent events can still produce different outcomes across habitats or population structures.

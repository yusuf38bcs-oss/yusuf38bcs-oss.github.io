# Ecological Succession, Disturbance, Stability and Resilience

This Lecture 18 forms part of the canonical 29-lecture LBFL Ecology course. It develops ecological succession, disturbance, stability and resilience through ecological mechanisms, evidence, interpretation and application.

## Learning Objectives

- Compare primary and secondary succession.
- Explain facilitation, inhibition, and tolerance models of succession.
- Analyze how disturbance regime changes successional trajectories.
- Compare classical climax ideas with a modern dynamic view of community change.

## 1. Succession

Ecological succession is directional change in community composition and structure through time. **Primary succession** begins where developed soil and biological legacies are minimal. **Secondary succession** follows disturbance where soil, seed banks, roots, microbes, or surviving organisms remain.

## 2. Mechanisms

In **facilitation**, early colonists modify the environment in ways that improve establishment by later species. In **inhibition**, established organisms reduce opportunities for later colonists. In the **tolerance** model, later species can establish without help from early colonists and eventually persist because they tolerate lower resource levels or compete effectively.

These mechanisms are not mutually exclusive.

## 3. Disturbance regime

Frequency, intensity, spatial extent, and timing of disturbance determine how succession proceeds. Repeated fire, grazing, flood, cyclone, erosion, or sediment deposition can reset or redirect community development.

## 4. From a single climax to dynamic trajectories

Classical ecology often emphasized a predictable march toward one climax community. Modern ecology places greater emphasis on historical contingency, dispersal limitation, stochastic colonization, repeated disturbance, and the possibility of alternative stable states.

## 5. Succession and ecosystem processes

As communities change, biomass, litter, soil organic matter, nutrient retention, productivity, and decomposition can also change. However, the direction and speed of these changes depend on the environment and the disturbance history.

## Integrated Perspective

### 3. Resistance, resilience, recovery, and transformation

**Resistance** is the capacity to change little during disturbance. **Resilience** is the capacity to regain structure or function after disturbance. **Recovery** is the observed trajectory of return. **Transformation** occurs when maintaining the previous state becomes impossible or undesirable and the system shifts toward a different configuration.

The literature uses these terms in different ways, so operational definitions should always be stated.

## Bangladesh Context

River chars, cyclone-affected coastal vegetation, abandoned fields, landslide scars, and newly accreted mudflats provide natural settings for studying succession under repeated disturbance.

## Application Exercise

Design a chronosequence using sites of different ages after disturbance. Choose four variables to measure and explain the main limitation of substituting space for time.

## Common Misconceptions

- Succession does not always follow one linear pathway to a single climax.
- Primary succession can still receive microbes and propagules from surrounding areas.

# Population Sampling: Quadrat, Mark–Recapture and Field Design

This Lecture 12 forms part of the canonical 29-lecture LBFL Ecology course. It develops population sampling: quadrat, mark–recapture and field design through ecological mechanisms, evidence, interpretation and application.


## Key Ideas

- Distinguish a census from a sample.
- Select random, systematic, or stratified sampling for different ecological questions.
- Choose among quadrats, line transects, and belt transects.
- Identify replication, independence, detectability, and sampling bias problems.

## 1. The sampling problem

Ecologists rarely count every organism in a population. They collect a sample and infer properties of a larger target population. A sound design defines the target population, sampling unit, sampling frame, and observation protocol before data collection begins.

## 2. Random sampling

Random selection gives eligible sampling units a known probability of inclusion and reduces selection bias. Random coordinates are commonly used for quadrat placement.

## 3. Systematic sampling

Systematic designs sample at fixed intervals. They are practical and can describe gradients well, but environmental periodicity can create bias if it matches the sampling interval.

## 4. Stratified sampling

When a habitat contains obvious subunits, such as salinity zones, forest types, or depth zones, each stratum can be sampled separately. Stratification often improves representation and precision.

## 5. Quadrats and transects

Quadrats are suitable for plants, sessile organisms, burrows, and other features that can be counted or measured in a fixed area. A line transect records contacts or observations along a line. A belt transect samples a fixed-width strip and is useful for gradients.

## 6. Replication and independence

Repeated measurements of the same experimental unit do not create independent replication. Pseudoreplication inflates apparent sample size. The ecological unit of inference must be defined clearly.

## 7. Detectability

Observed abundance depends on both true abundance and detection. Vegetation density, observer skill, weather, time of day, and animal behaviour can change detectability and bias comparisons.

## Integrated Perspective

### 1. Mark–recapture logic

Suppose <var>M</var> individuals are captured, marked, and released. In a later sample, <var>C</var> individuals are captured and <var>R</var> of them are marked. If marked animals have mixed back into the population and capture probabilities are comparable, the marked proportion in the second sample can be used to estimate total population size.

### 2. Assumptions

A simple Lincoln–Petersen estimate assumes that the population is approximately closed during the study, marks are not lost, marking does not alter survival or behaviour, marked individuals mix with unmarked individuals, and capture probabilities are not severely unequal.

Violating these assumptions can produce substantial bias.

## Bangladesh Context

Mangrove vegetation can be stratified by salinity zone, wetland birds by habitat type, crop pests by field block, and aquatic plants by depth. Seasonal accessibility can bias sampling if difficult sites are systematically omitted.

## Application and Practice

Design a 30-sample campus tree survey. Choose random, systematic, or stratified sampling and explain how roadsides, pond edges, and interior green spaces affect the design.

## Common Misconceptions

- A large sample cannot correct a systematically biased design.
- Repeated measurements are not always independent replicates.

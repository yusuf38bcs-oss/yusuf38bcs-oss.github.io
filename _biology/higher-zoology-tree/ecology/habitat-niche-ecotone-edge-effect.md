# Habitat, Ecological Niche, Ecotone and Edge Effect

This Lecture 03 forms part of the canonical 29-lecture LBFL Ecology course. It develops habitat, ecological niche, ecotone and edge effect through ecological mechanisms, evidence, interpretation and application.

## Learning Objectives

- Distinguish habitat from ecological niche.
- Explain fundamental and realized niches.
- Interpret niche breadth and overlap.
- Apply the concept of ecological equivalents in biogeographic comparisons.

## 1. Habitat and niche are not the same

A habitat is the environment in which an organism lives. An ecological niche is the multidimensional set of conditions, resources, interactions, and functional roles that permit a population to persist. The common “address versus profession” analogy is useful for beginners, but a niche includes much more than a job.

## 2. Hutchinson's niche concept

A niche can be represented as an n-dimensional hypervolume. Dimensions can include temperature, salinity, prey size, nesting substrate, water depth, activity time, and other biologically relevant variables.

## 3. Fundamental and realized niche

The **fundamental niche** represents the range a species could occupy based on its physiological and resource requirements in the absence of restrictive biotic interactions. The **realized niche** reflects the range actually occupied after competition, predation, disease, dispersal constraints, and other interactions are considered.

## 4. Niche breadth and overlap

Generalists use a broad range of resources or conditions; specialists use a narrower range. High overlap creates the potential for competition, but overlap alone does not prove competition. Resources may be abundant or interactions may be weak.

## 5. Ecological equivalents

Organisms in different regions can occupy similar functional roles even when they are not closely related. Similar environmental pressures can therefore produce convergent ecological strategies.

## Integrated Perspective

### 5. Ecotones and edge effects

An **ecotone** is a transition between ecological communities or ecosystem types. Edges can differ from interiors in light, wind, temperature, predator access, recruitment, and species composition. Edge effects can be positive or negative. It is therefore unsafe to assume that every edge automatically increases biodiversity.

## Bangladesh Context

Wetland conversion can reduce habitat area while also changing food, nesting sites, hydrology, disturbance, and microclimate. It therefore affects multiple niche dimensions at once.

## Application Exercise

Build a five-dimensional niche profile for a familiar bird, fish, or insect using food, microhabitat, climate, activity time, and breeding site. Identify the dimension most vulnerable to disturbance.

## Common Misconceptions

- A niche is not simply a physical place; habitat is closer to that concept.
- Niche overlap does not by itself prove competitive exclusion.

4. Design a field observation or experiment that could test one claim from this lecture.

# Biodiversity Loss, Extinction, Invasive Species and Fragmentation

This Lecture 27 forms part of the canonical 29-lecture LBFL Ecology course. It develops biodiversity loss, extinction, invasive species and fragmentation through ecological mechanisms, evidence, interpretation and application.

## Learning Objectives

- Explain major anthropogenic drivers of biodiversity loss and ecosystem change.
- Compare in situ and ex situ conservation approaches.
- Evaluate protected-area design using representation, connectivity, governance, and monitoring.
- Interpret threatened-species categories and biodiversity-hotspot concepts carefully.

## 1. Applied ecology

Applied ecology uses ecological theory and evidence to address practical problems such as habitat loss, overharvest, invasive species, pollution, urbanization, restoration, wildlife management, and sustainable resource use.

## 2. Land-use change and fragmentation

Conversion of forest, wetland, or grassland reduces habitat area. When remaining habitat becomes small and isolated, edge effects increase and movement between patches can decline. Species responses depend on mobility, body size, specialization, and the permeability of the surrounding matrix.

## 5. Threat categories and biodiversity hotspots

Threat categories are structured assessments of extinction risk, not simple descriptions of rarity. A biodiversity hotspot is not merely a place with many species; the formal concept combines exceptional endemism with severe habitat loss.

## 6. Urban ecology

Cities are ecosystems with heat islands, artificial light, altered hydrology, fragmented green space, pollution, and novel species interactions. Ecological planning can improve connectivity, cooling, water retention, and habitat quality.

## Integrated Perspective

### 3. Connectivity

**Structural connectivity** describes visible physical connection on a map. **Functional connectivity** asks whether an organism can actually move through the landscape. A narrow road gap may be trivial for a bird and a serious barrier for an amphibian.

### 4. Disturbance and geomorphology

Flood, cyclone, fire, erosion, landslide, and sedimentation create and rearrange patches. Natural disturbance is not automatically ecological damage. Many systems depend on a characteristic disturbance regime, but altered frequency or intensity can push them beyond their historical range.

### 5. Invasion

Roads, rivers, shipping routes, and disturbed edges can increase propagule movement. Establishment depends on propagule pressure, resource availability, disturbance, biotic resistance, and species traits.

## Integrated Perspective

## 5. Pollution ecology

Organic pollutants include many hydrocarbons, pesticides, and persistent organic compounds. Inorganic pollutants include metals, excess nutrients, salts, and other non-carbon-based contaminants. Toxicity depends on dose, chemical form, persistence, bioavailability, and exposure route.

## 6. Bioaccumulation and biomagnification

**Bioaccumulation** occurs when uptake exceeds elimination within an organism. **Biomagnification** refers to increasing concentration of certain persistent chemicals at higher trophic levels. Not every pollutant biomagnifies.

## Bangladesh Context

Conservation problems in Bangladesh include mangrove management, haor wetlands, hill forests, migratory-bird habitat, river biodiversity, and urban green space. Effective plans must consider ecological evidence together with livelihood and governance realities.

## Application Exercise

Create a management matrix for a wetland with encroachment, nutrient pollution, fishing pressure, and an invasive plant. For each threat, list a pressure indicator, response indicator, intervention, and monitoring metric.

## Common Misconceptions

- Declaring a protected area does not guarantee biodiversity recovery.
- Conservation does not always require excluding people from ecosystems.
